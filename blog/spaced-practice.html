Why a little every day beats a lot once a week

Short sessions spread over many days suit how memory works. This is what we took from the research, and where it runs out.

Bonet is a practice tool, not a medical device, and this post is about design choices. Research informed them. We have no data of our own showing that the app improves anyone's speech, memory or health, and we do not claim it.

The gap between sessions matters

A large review of lab studies on verbal recall pooled 839 assessments and found that spaced practice beats massed practice. It also found that the best gap grows with how long you need to remember something (Cepeda et al., 2006). We read only the abstract of that paper. It does not tell us how many of the participants were older adults, so we treat it as general evidence about memory and not as a result for our users.

For someone who does ten minutes in the kitchen after breakfast, this is a practical point. Seven short visits give memory seven chances to be used. One long visit gives it one.

Pulling a word out beats looking at it again

Spacing works together with retrieval. In a well-known pair of experiments, students who took a memory test on material remembered more after a delay than students who simply studied it again (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). We have the abstract and a secondary summary, not the full paper.

The speaking part of Bonet follows this. The app shows a picture or reads a prompt, and the person tries to say the word before seeing it. How we handle the times when it will not come is the subject of our post on the hint ladder.

What we know about older adults

One study of older adults and people with early Alzheimer's disease compared expanding gaps with equal gaps. Any spaced retrieval helped. Expanding gaps gave no reliable advantage over equal ones, and the difference had disappeared after a 24-hour delay (Balota et al., 2006). We have only a secondary summary of that paper.

Spaced retrieval training is also used with people who have dementia. A professional-body summary rates its evidence as class II and III, and a meta-analysis of 35 studies reports an effect on semantic memory (Hopper et al.; Oren et al., 2014). We have seen both only as summaries. The studies are small, and they are clinician-led training, not a phone app.

Because the exact schedule seems to matter less than retrieving with some gap, we did not try to tune it. The gaps in the app are our own choices.

How the app spaces practice

Every word or phrase in speaking practice carries one of four labels: New, Learning, Familiar or Very familiar (in the Chinese app, 新, 學緊, 熟 and 好熟). An item that someone says without help moves up one step, and the app schedules it to come back after 1, 3, 7, 14 and then 30 days. An item can move up only once in a calendar day. Reaching Familiar therefore takes an unaided answer on three different days, and no amount of repeating it on one afternoon changes that.

A wrong answer moves the item back one step at most, never to New. An item answered correctly only after a bigger hint stays where it is. Words that are due come back in a short Review of up to six items, about three minutes, and the first one is always the item the person knows best.

Each level also shows one to three paw prints. One paw means the level was passed. Two paws mean at least three quarters of its items are Familiar or better, and three paws mean the same share is Very familiar. Paw prints are only ever added. A level that has gone quiet for a month does not lose them, and the Practise again button on a finished level cannot lower anything.

The gaps, the three-quarters share and the six-item Review are judgement values. They are shorter and flatter than the gaps we have seen in adult language apps, because we judged that older people need more frequent retrieval. That judgement is ours and has not been tested. No speech-language therapist has reviewed them yet, and we expect to change them.

The streak counts days, not scores

The streak rule in the app takes only the dates on which someone practised. It never sees a score. A day with a hard session counts the same as a day with an easy one. The app also gives two streak savers, which cover missed days, and a new one is earned after every seventh day.

We chose this partly because of something Duolingo has said about its own users: people who binge are much more likely to quit (Duolingo). Duolingo gave no figures there, and its learners are not our users, so this is a reason to be careful, not proof.

Where the evidence stops

Most of the spacing research we cite uses lab tasks and recall tests. How well it carries over to saying a word at the bus stop is not something these studies measure. Motor-learning work on speech makes a related warning: how well someone does during practice is not the same as what they retain afterwards (Maas et al., 2008). That is why the app counts independent answers on different days and not only the score in one session.

The Brain games follow the same caution. People get better at the tasks they practise, but evidence that this carries over to everyday memory and thinking is weak (Simons et al., 2016). We therefore describe those games as short practice, and we make no claim about everyday life.
