Designing for older adults: big text, one button, no punishment

Most of these choices come from experience with the app and common accessibility guidance, and we say where research is thin.

Bonet is made for older adults who practise at home, some of them after a stroke. That shapes the screen more than any feature does. Much of what follows is design judgement, not research.

Text size and button size

The app has three text sizes: normal (18 px), large (20 px) and extra large (22.4 px). Type and spacing are set in rem units, so the whole screen scales, not only the words. Our largest setting is about a quarter bigger than the default. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines ask that text can be resized up to 200 percent without loss of content at level AA (W3C, 2023a), so we have not reached that bar through this setting alone, and we have not audited the app against WCAG. We use it as a reference for what to aim at.

For targets, WCAG 2.2 sets 24 by 24 CSS pixels at level AA (W3C, 2023b) and 44 by 44 at the stricter level AAA (W3C, 2023c). Our shared buttons run from about 50 px for the smallest to about 81 px for the big Start button, measured at the default text size. We made them large for an unsteady hand, which is our reasoning and not a study's finding.

One next step

Simple mode is for a person using the app alone. It cuts the app to three tabs. The Today screen shows one card with the next activity, one large Start button, a count such as "1 of 3 done today", and a quieter "See everything" button. Turning it on also raises the text to large unless you already chose bigger. A helper who sets the app up for someone else is not asked about it.

Simple mode also hides things that ask for attention. The league is off unless you switch it on in Settings, the streak is small, your email address is hidden on screen until you choose otherwise, and locked Premium items are simply not listed. Brain games open at a level chosen for you.

Streaks that bend

A streak counts days in a row. We give you up to two streak savers, and you earn one back for every seven days in a row. A saver covers one missed day, and the calendar marks it so you can see what happened. If the streak does end, the message reads: "That is fine, everyone has breaks. Today is a fresh start." The streak does drop to zero when you miss more days than you have savers, so it is not free of loss.

The design borrows an idea from Duolingo, which has published that learners who binge are much more likely to quit, and it offers features such as a weekend amulet to ease streak pressure (Duolingo, n.d.). Duolingo's users are not older adults, and the post gives no figures for that claim, so we treat it as a prompt and not as proof. The plan bends too: after a break it gets lighter, and the app does not count missed days out loud.

Levels without failing grades

A speaking level opens the next one when 60 percent of answers are given without help. That pass mark is our judgement, and a speech-language therapist has yet to review it. Missing it takes nothing away. You can practise the level again, and paw prints on a level are only ever added. Messages are short and plain: "That one is still new. We'll see it again soon." A memory game that goes wrong says "Look again next time".

Brain games need a word of caution. People get better at the games they practise, but evidence that this carries over to everyday thinking is weak (Simons et al., 2016). We never say a game improves memory. For the first two sessions of a game we treat the scores as familiarisation, and a rising trend is worded as something that "may partly be practice".

The weekly league

The weekly league is optional. The join screen says: "Practise at your own pace. This is just for fun." Others see only a nickname and weekly points, never your name or email, and leaving deletes the nickname and results.

A week runs from Monday to Sunday. You are placed in a group of up to 30 players in the same league (ten leagues, from Seed to Sun). Points come from your sessions: at most 100 for one session, at most 8 sessions a day count, and at most 300 a day, so a long single session cannot win the week. In a group of 8 or more, the top quarter (up to 7) with some points move up. Only in a group of 12 or more real players do the bottom fifth (up to 5) move down, so small groups never push anyone down.

When fewer than 10 real people are in a group, computer players fill it to 10 rows. The "About the league" sheet says so, and so does our privacy policy. They never take a real player's promotion place and never cause a demotion, but a real player must also match the lower median of their scores to move up. This is a product decision with a cost: players appear as nicknames, and we are ready to switch it off if people object. We also have not made points tamper-proof, because the app reports them from your own device.

What we left out

The app has no third-party advertising or analytics. There are no hearts or lives to lose, and no scores compared with other people unless you join the league. We make no medical claims: Bonet is a practice tool, not a medical device, and it has not been through a clinical trial. We found no reliable research on whether leaderboards or loss mechanics harm older adults, so "no punishment" is our design principle and not an evidence-based rule.

One thing we did not leave out is the clock. The memory pairs game has a time limit of 90 to 150 seconds and shows the time left, and the "keep going" variants of some games run for a few minutes. We may change both.
