How the hint ladder works in speaking practice

When a word will not come, the app gives a small hint first and a bigger one only if it is needed. This is how the steps are ordered and what we can and cannot say about them.

Bonet is a practice tool, not a medical device or therapy. No speech-language therapist has reviewed the ladder yet, and we do not claim that it improves anyone's speech. What follows describes what the app does and the research behind some of the choices.

The five steps

Take a picture of a bowl of congee in the naming mode. The person is asked what it is, and the word does not come. The app has up to five steps ready, in this order.

  • Step 1 is to listen again. The prompt is repeated, more slowly.
  • Step 2 gives the first sound (in Cantonese, the first character).
  • Step 3 gives a meaning clue, such as the category "food", or removes two of the answer choices.
  • Step 4 shows the written word.
  • Step 5 plays the word, and the person says it along with the app.

Not every item has every step. A one-letter answer has no useful first sound, and an item whose written word is already on screen has nothing to gain from step 4. After a wrong answer the app offers the next step on its own, and the person can ask for one at any time with the hint button.

Why the steps go from small to big

Speech-language therapists have long used cueing hierarchies in naming work, and our own review notes describe cue-based naming as a core part of that practice. Those notes are an internal document written by us, not a publication, and no therapist has checked them. They are why we built the ladder this way, not evidence that it works.

Our reasoning is simple, and it is reasoning, not a finding. A hint that is too big takes over the job of finding the word. A listen-again prompt leaves the person doing all of the work. So the ladder gives one step at a time, and the person has another go before the next step arrives.

That fits what retrieval research says. Students who tried to recall material remembered more after a delay than students who studied it again (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). We have read the abstract and a secondary summary of that study, which was done with students, not with people who have aphasia.

Errorless and effortful practice

One school of therapy tries to prevent errors, on the idea that a wrong attempt strengthens the wrong answer. A review of that approach exists (Fillingham et al., 2003), and the evidence since has been mixed. In a study of verb and noun naming, an errorless method was as effective as a more errorful cueing method (Conroy et al., 2009). We read the abstract of that study ourselves. A secondary summary we saw says both approaches help most people.

A 2016 study went the other way. Retrieval practice beat errorless repetition, and spaced practice beat massed practice, at a test one day later and a follow-up a week later (Middleton et al., 2016). The abstract says four people took part. A result from four people is weak, and we say so.

With the evidence this mixed, we do not call the app errorless. Every attempt can fail. What it offers is effortful retrieval with support that grows one step at a time. That is a design choice, and we describe it as one.

What the person sees

A wrong answer gets the words "Not quite yet" and the next hint. There is no failing grade and no counter of mistakes. When the ladder runs out, the app says that one is tricky, thanks the person for trying and shows the answer. The item is then recorded at the highest step it needed, and it comes back in a later Review.

The record does separate help from no help. An item counts as independent if it was right with no hint or only a listen-again. This matters for two things. A level opens when the independent share of a session reaches 60 out of 100, and below that the screen says to try again tomorrow or now. And a word moves from Learning towards Familiar only when it is answered without a bigger hint. A word got right at step 4 stays where it was, and a wrong answer moves it back by at most one step. These thresholds are our judgement values, and a speech-language therapist needs to review them.

When a session needed fewer hints than the last one, the app says so in plain words. It also shows the average hint step in the therapist report, so that someone who works with the person can see the trend. A shorter ladder from one session to the next is a practice measure that comes from the app's own recogniser, and nothing more.

What we leave out

We do not claim that smaller hints first give better results than bigger ones. We found no study in our notes that tests that for this ladder. The Cantonese first-character step is a design choice, and our notes record no treatment study of it. Speech recognition on disordered speech and on Cantonese is also limited, so the app can mark a right answer as wrong. For that reason a miss costs one step at most.

People who are working with a therapist should follow the therapist's cues. The app is extra repetition between sessions.
